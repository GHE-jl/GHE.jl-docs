Spatial superposition

A single-borehole g-function describes one isolated source. In a borehole field the boreholes interact thermally: each one warms (or cools) the ground around its neighbours, so the field response is larger than that of an isolated borehole. Spatial superposition combines the single-borehole responses at every pairwise distance into one effective field g-function.

The output is always normalised to 1 W/m on every borehole and returned in °C·m/W, whatever the boundary condition. The internal space–time solve produces the borefield g-function ḡ(t) for a unit step in time; load-side temporal superposition (the convolution of ḡ with an actual time-varying load) is applied separately, downstream of this package.

The three boundary conditions

How the heat load is allowed to distribute among, and along, the boreholes defines the boundary condition of the superposition. The package provides the three classical choices, in order of increasing detail, each with its own function:

Boundary conditionPhysical assumptionFunction
BC-IUniform heat fluxEvery borehole emits the same flux; no coupling constraintbc_I
BC-IIUniform flux per borehole, equal mean temperatureFlux is uniform along each borehole; the mean wall temperature is equal across boreholes (parallel connection)bc_II
BC-IIIUniform borehole-wall temperatureFlux varies within each borehole so the wall temperature is uniform over the whole fieldbc_III

They bracket the true field response, with BC-I ≥ BC-II ≥ BC-III at any given time (a finer boundary condition lets the field shed heat more efficiently, lowering the g-function). BC-I and BC-II coincide for a field whose boreholes are all geometrically equivalent (e.g. a symmetric 2×2 grid), since the fluxes then stay equal.

Under BC-II and BC-III, the 1 W/m is the average over the field: the flux of each borehole (or segment) is an unknown, different from one borehole to the next and changing in time. A corner borehole, for instance, takes a larger share of the load than a central one once the boreholes interact. That is why these two conditions superpose the load history in time as well as in space.

The pairwise-geometry matrices

The raw ingredient of every method is the $n_b \times n_b$ matrix of distances between every pair of boreholes, with the diagonal set to the borehole radius $r_b$ (a borehole's distance "to itself" is its own wall). borefield_geometry builds this matrix from the coordinate array xy, together with the flow-relative angle matrix $\theta$ (in degrees, diagonal $0$). The isotropic models use only $r$, and the moving models (MILS, MFLS) also use $\theta$ because advection makes the pairwise response asymmetric. Evaluating a model at these matrices yields the $n_t \times n_b \times n_b$ g-array that the superposition methods consume:

\[g_{ij}(t) = g\bigl(r_{ij}, \theta_{ij}, t\bigr), \qquad r_{ij} = \begin{cases} \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert & i \ne j \\ r_b & i = j, \end{cases}\]

where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle of the source→receiver direction relative to the flow ($+x$); for the isotropic models the $\theta$ dependence drops out.

BC-I — uniform flux

bc_I (the M1 model of Guo et al., 2021) is the simplest scheme: every borehole emits the same constant flux, and the field g-function is the average over all boreholes of each borehole's self-plus-mutual response:

\[\bar g(t) = \frac{1}{n_b} \sum_{i=1}^{n_b} \sum_{j=1}^{n_b} g_{ij}(t).\]

It requires no linear solve, which makes it numerically robust even for the strongly asymmetric response matrices produced by the advection models. It is the upper bound of the three and the scheme Guo et al. (2021) validated against a 3-D finite-element model with groundwater flow.

BC-II and BC-III — time marching

BC-II adds the coupling constraint that all boreholes reach the same mean wall temperature (as happens when they are connected in parallel to a common header), while the flux is still uniform along each borehole. BC-III (Cimmino & Bernier, 2014) divides each borehole into nseg segments and lets the flux vary from segment to segment, so that the wall temperature is uniform over the whole field and constant with depth. With nseg = 1 the two are the same condition, and they are solved by the same algorithm over $n_S$ sources: the $n_b$ boreholes for bc_II, or the $n_b \cdot n_{seg}$ segments for bc_III. The segment responses use the segment-to-segment finite line source, the general fls kernel with independent emitting $(H_1, D_1)$ and receiving $(H_2, D_2)$ segments.

The flux of each source is constant over each time step, so the unknowns of step $p$ are the flux increments $f_{j,p} = q_j(t_p) - q_j(t_{p-1})$ and the common wall temperature $T_p$. The temperature of source $i$ superposes the response to every increment since $t = 0$:

\[T_i(t_p) = \sum_{j} \sum_{l=1}^{p} g_{ij}(t_p - t_{l-1})\, f_{j,l} = \sum_{j} g_{ij}(t_p - t_{p-1})\, f_{j,p} + h_{i,p}, \qquad h_{i,p} = \sum_{l<p} \sum_{j} g_{ij}(t_p - t_{l-1})\, f_{j,l},\]

where the load history $h_{i,p}$ only involves the increments of earlier steps, already known. With one common temperature ($T_i(t_p) = T_p$ for every $i$) and a constant total heat rate ($\sum_j H_j f_{j,p} = H_{ref}$ at $p = 1$, $0$ afterwards), each step solves a small $(n_S + 1) \times (n_S + 1)$ system:

\[\begin{bmatrix} g(t_p - t_{p-1}) & -\mathbf{1} \\ \mathbf{H}^\top & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{f}_p \\ T_p \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf{h}_p \\ H_{ref}\,\delta_{p1} \end{bmatrix}.\]

The g-function is $\bar g(t_p) = T_p$ (with $H_{ref}$ the total length, i.e. 1 W/m per borehole). This is a direct solution: no iteration and no tolerance. Stacking every step gives the single block system of Dusseault et al. (2018), which is lower block-triangular in time; time marching is its forward substitution, so both give the same solution, at a cost of $n_t\, n_S^3$ (one factorisation) plus $n_t^2 n_S^2 / 2$ for the history instead of $(n_t\, n_S)^3$. On a uniform grid $t_k = k\,\Delta t$ the lags are themselves grid times, $g(t_p - t_{l-1}) = g(t_{p-l+1})$, so only $g$ at the requested times is needed and the step matrix $g(\Delta t)$ is factorised once.

The forward substitution multiplies rounding errors by $g(t_k)/g(\Delta t)$ at every step. It is therefore unstable when the first step lies in the onset of the self response ($\Delta t \lesssim r_b^2/\alpha$, below about one hour for usual boreholes), where $g$ grows by orders of magnitude from one step to the next. Heat has then not left the vicinity of the borehole wall: the sources do not interact and their fluxes stay uniform, so the uniform-flux (BC-I) value is returned for such a block (detected when a self response grows more than tenfold over the first step). The legacy BC-III solvers have no such safeguard and are wrong on those blocks.

Time sampling

Both BC-II and BC-III therefore need a uniform grid $t = \Delta t, 2\Delta t, \dots, n\Delta t$. With interp = true (the default), the g-function is solved on internal uniform blocks — hourly to one day, daily to one month, monthly to one year, quarterly to ten years and yearly to fifty years (decades beyond) — following the geometric progression of Cimmino et al. (2013) and Nguyen & Pasquier (2021), and the nodes are interpolated to the requested t by a monotone cubic (PCHIP) in $\log t$. This keeps the cost to about two hundred solved times whatever the length of t. Each block restarts from $t = 0$ with its own step, so the flux redistribution during its first step is not resolved; the quarterly and yearly blocks keep that error to about 0.2 % for a 100-borehole field. Pass interp = false to solve directly on a t that is already $\Delta t, 2\Delta t, \dots$ (any other t is rejected). For BC-I interp is a performance choice only.

Scope and cost

BC-III is implemented for the finite line source (vertical boreholes). Memory grows as $n_t (n_b\, n_{seg})^2$, so it grows quickly with field size and segment count; a handful of segments (nseg = 2–12) is usually enough for converged g-functions.

Selecting the boundary condition

ground_response selects the boundary condition with its bc keyword; the number of segments is a field of FLSModel, and BC-III is the default when it is larger than one:

xy = borefield(:rectangle, 5, 5, 5.0)

g_bc1 = ground_response(t, rb, xy, FLSModel(150.0, 4.0, ks, Cs); bc = :I)   # BC-I
g_bc2 = ground_response(t, rb, xy, FLSModel(150.0, 4.0, ks, Cs))            # BC-II (nseg = 1)
g_bc3 = ground_response(t, rb, xy, FLSModel(150.0, 4.0, ks, Cs, 8))         # BC-III, 8 segments

Two ways to call each method

Every method accepts either a precomputed g-array (low-level) or borefield parameters with a model (high-level):

# High-level: pass coordinates and a model — the geometry and g-array are built internally
g = bc_I(t, rb, xy, MILSModel(rb, ks, Cs, Cf, vD))
g = bc_II(t, rb, xy, FLSModel(150.0, 4.0, ks, Cs))
g = bc_III(t, rb, xy, FLSModel(150.0, 4.0, ks, Cs, 8))

# Low-level: pass a precomputed g-array (any model, or a custom one) on t = Δt, 2Δt, …
tu  = 2_592_000.0 .* (1:12)                # monthly steps
r   = borefield_geometry(xy, rb)[1]        # nb × nb distance matrix
g3D = fls(tu, r, 150.0, 4.0, ks, Cs)       # nt × nb × nb
g   = bc_II(g3D)

A single method is the extension point for custom models: a new AbstractGroundModel becomes usable across bc_I, bc_II and ground_response by defining GroundResponse._borehole_response(t, rb, xy, m), which returns the pairwise nt × nb × nb response array (see Overview).

Legacy solvers

The solvers used before version 2.2 are kept under a _legacy suffix for comparison; their former names still work as deprecated aliases. All of them need the same uniform time grid (provided by interp = true).

  • successive_flux_legacy (BC-II, Nguyen & Pasquier, 2021) iterates on the fluxes of every borehole at every time until the wall temperatures agree within 0.1 %, with one convolution per pair of boreholes. It converges towards the bc_II solution, within about $10^{-5}$ to $3 \times 10^{-4}$.
  • bloc_matrix_legacy (BC-II, Dusseault et al., 2018) and segment_response_legacy (BC-III) solve the whole space–time block system at once. They give the same solution as bc_II and bc_III, at a cost growing as $(n_t\, n_S)^3$.
  • segment_response_marching_legacy (BC-III) is the marching algorithm reading each lag of the load history as a strided slice of g, which is slower on large problems.
Correction in 2.2

Before version 2.2, the block solvers (bloc_matrix, segment_response) filled column $l$ of each time block with $g(t_k)$ instead of $g(t_k - t_{l-1})$, which drops the load history: each time step was solved as if the current fluxes had been applied since $t = 0$. The error was zero on a symmetric field and reached a few percent once the fluxes redistribute. The legacy block solvers are corrected.
